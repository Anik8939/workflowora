Debt-to-Income (DTI) Ratio Guide: The 28/36 Rule US Lenders Use to Approve Mortgages

Even if you have a 780 credit score, a bank will reject your mortgage or personal loan application if your Debt-to-Income (DTI) ratio exceeds their underwriting ceiling. Understanding how underwriters calculate Front-End and Back-End DTI lets you know your exact borrowing power before a lender runs a hard credit pull.

1. The Exact Debt-to-Income (DTI) Formula

Your DTI compares your total required monthly debt payments against your gross (pre-tax) monthly income—never your net take-home pay.

Back-End DTI Ratio Formula:
DTI % = (Total Monthly Minimum Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100

What counts as monthly debt? Proposed mortgage principal, interest, property taxes, homeowner's insurance, HOA dues (PITI), auto loan EMIs, federal/private student loan payments, personal loan installments, and the minimum required monthly payment shown on your credit card statements. Utilities, groceries, health insurance, and 401(k) contributions are excluded.

2. The Classic 28/36 Rule Explained

Most conventional US lenders benchmark borrowers against the 28/36 qualifying ratio:

  • 28% Front-End Ratio (Housing Ratio): Your total monthly housing payment (Principal + Interest + Taxes + Insurance) should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income. On a $96,000/year salary ($8,000/month gross), your max ideal housing payment is $2,240/month.
  • 36% Back-End Ratio (Total Debt Ratio): Your housing payment plus all other recurring debt payments (car loans, student loans, credit cards) should stay at or below 36% ($2,880/month on an $8,000 gross monthly income).

4. Fastest Way to Drop Your DTI Before Applying

Because lenders only care about your required monthly minimum payment (not the total loan balance), paying off a small car loan that has $1,800 left at a $450/month payment lowers your DTI 9x more effectively than putting $1,800 toward a large student loan with a $50/month payment.
