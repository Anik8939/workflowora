Debt-to-Income (DTI) Ratio and the 28/36 Rule for Mortgage Underwriting

Why Your Credit Score Alone Will Not Approve a Mortgage

First-time homebuyers frequently walk into loan pre-approval with a 760 credit score and a healthy down payment, only to get blindsided by a smaller loan ceiling than they expected. The culprit is almost always their Debt-to-Income (DTI) ratio. While your credit score measures historical reliability, DTI measures monthly cash-flow capacity—telling the underwriter how much of your gross pre-tax paycheck is already spoken for by recurring debt obligations.

Mortgage lenders examine two separate DTI ratios side by side, anchored around the classic 28/36 underwriting guideline and the federal 43% Qualified Mortgage (QM) benchmark.

Front-End vs. Back-End DTI Equations

Front-End DTI (%) = (Proposed Monthly Housing PITI + HOA ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100
Back-End DTI (%) = (Housing PITI + Auto + Student + Card Minimums + Alimony ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100

Notice two critical underwriting conventions: lenders always divide by gross (pre-tax) monthly income, not net take-home pay, and they exclude everyday living expenses like groceries, utilities, health insurance premiums, and streaming subscriptions—focusing strictly on contractual credit-bureau and court-ordered obligations.

Worked Underwriting Scenario: $8,500 Gross Monthly Household Income

Suppose a household earns $102,000 per year ($8,500/month gross) and applies for a conventional mortgage with a projected PITI + HOA payment of $2,150/month:

  • Proposed Housing (Principal, Interest, Property Tax, Insurance, HOA): $2,150
  • Auto Loan Payment: $420
  • Student Loan Payment: $250
  • Minimum Credit Card Payments (sum of minimums across all open cards): $130
  • Total Monthly Debt Obligations: $2,150 + $420 + $250 + $130 = $2,950

Computing both ratios: Front-End DTI = $2,150 ÷ $8,500 = 25.3% (comfortably below 28%). Back-End DTI = $2,950 ÷ $8,500 = 34.7% (below 36%). This borrower qualifies cleanly for prime conventional pricing with $110/month of additional buffer under the 36% cap.

Fastest Legal Ways to Improve DTI 60 Days Before Applying

  1. Eliminate High-Payment / Low-Balance Installment Loans: Paying off a $3,200 remaining car loan that carries a $420/month payment removes $420 from your DTI numerator immediately—freeing up roughly $63,000 in mortgage purchasing power at 6.75%.
  2. Pay Down Revolving Cards Before Statement Closing: Lowering statement balances drops minimum required payments reported to Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion.
  3. Avoid Opening Any New Credit Lines: Financing furniture or leasing a vehicle during underwriting triggers a credit refresh and can push Back-End DTI over the threshold days before closing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do lenders count my full credit card balance or just the minimum payment in DTI?

Lenders count only the minimum monthly payment shown on your credit report—not the full statement balance—even if you pay the card in full every month.

How do underwriters treat self-employed 1099 income for DTI?

Lenders typically average the last two years of net Schedule C profit (after business deductions) from your federal tax returns, not gross revenue. Heavy tax write-offs lower taxable income but also reduce mortgage borrowing power.

What if a student loan is in deferment or $0 IDR payment?

Fannie Mae and FHA have specific fallback rules: if your credit report shows $0 due, underwriters often impute 0.5% to 1.0% of the total student loan balance as a monthly payment unless you provide written Income-Driven Repayment documentation.

Is 43% Back-End DTI actually comfortable to live on?

Usually not. Remember DTI uses pre-tax income. After 22%–28% in federal, state, and payroll taxes, a 43% gross DTI can consume over 60% of your actual net take-home paycheck.
