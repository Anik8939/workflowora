APR vs. APY Explained: Why Compounding Frequency Changes What You Earn and Pay

Why Banks Advertise APY on Savings Accounts and APR on Loans

Open any banking comparison page and you will notice an unmistakable pattern: high-yield savings accounts and certificates of deposit headline their APY (Annual Percentage Yield), whereas credit cards, auto loans, and mortgages headline their APR (Annual Percentage Rate). That split is not accidental. Because APY includes the effect of intra-year compounding, APY is always equal to or higher than APR—making deposit rates look larger and borrowing rates look smaller.

Understanding the exact mathematical bridge between nominal APR and effective APY prevents costly miscalculations when comparing two savings accounts with different compounding intervals or evaluating the true annual cost of a high-interest credit line.

Exact Formula Converting APR to APY

APY = (1 + APR ÷ n)^n − 1

Here, APR is expressed as a decimal (e.g., 12% = 0.12) and n is the number of compounding periods per year (1 for annual, 4 for quarterly, 12 for monthly, 365 for daily).

Worked Examples: Same Rate, Different Compounding Frequencies

First, consider a 5.00% nominal rate on a $25,000 savings deposit:

  • Annual compounding (n = 1): APY = (1 + 0.05)^1 − 1 = 5.000% ($1,250.00 Year-1 interest).
  • Monthly compounding (n = 12): APY = (1 + 0.05/12)^12 − 1 = 5.116% ($1,279.05 Year-1 interest).
  • Daily compounding (n = 365): APY = (1 + 0.05/365)^365 − 1 = 5.127% ($1,281.69 Year-1 interest).

At 5%, daily compounding adds a modest 0.127 percentage points. Now look at what happens on a 24.00% APR credit card compounded daily:

Effective APY = (1 + 0.24 / 365)^365 − 1 = 27.115%! At high borrowing rates, daily compounding quietly adds more than 3.11 full percentage points to your true annual cost of debt if balances roll over unpaid.

Mortgage APR Note: Fees Folded Into the Rate

In US mortgage disclosures (under the Truth in Lending Act), Mortgage APR is slightly different from the note interest rate: it rolls origination fees, discount points, and certain closing costs into an annualized percentage so borrowers can compare two lenders on an apples-to-apples basis. A 6.25% note rate with $6,000 in lender points might show a 6.52% APR—signaling that the lower headline rate costs more upfront if you plan to sell or refinance within five years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which should I maximize when choosing a savings account: APR or APY?

Always compare APY for savings accounts, CDs, and money market funds because APY already standardizes compounding frequency into one true annual yield.

Can APY ever be lower than APR?

Mathematically no, assuming a positive interest rate and compounding frequency of at least once per year. They are equal only when interest compounds once annually (n = 1).

How much difference does daily vs. monthly compounding make on a 5% savings account?

On $10,000 at 5% nominal rate, daily compounding earns $512.67 in year one versus $511.62 for monthly compounding—just $1.05 difference. The headline rate matters far more than daily vs. monthly compounding at low single-digit yields.

Does the Rule of 72 use APR or APY?

Use APY (your effective annual compounding return) when dividing 72 by your annual return to estimate how many years it takes for an investment to double.
