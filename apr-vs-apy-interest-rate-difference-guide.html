APR vs. APY Explained: Why Banks Use Two Different Interest Rates for Loans and Savings

Have you ever noticed that banks advertise APR (Annual Percentage Rate) when they want you to borrow money on a credit card or auto loan, but advertise APY (Annual Percentage Yield) when they want you to deposit money into a High-Yield Savings Account or CD? That is not an accident—it is simple compounding math working in the bank's favor.

1. The Core Difference Between APR and APY

  • APR (Annual Percentage Rate): The simple stated annual interest rate without accounting for intra-year compounding (Periodic Rate × Number of Periods in a Year). Lenders prefer showing APR because it looks smaller than the true compounded cost.
  • APY (Annual Percentage Yield / EAR): The actual effective annual return after accounting for daily, monthly, or quarterly compound interest. Banks highlight APY on savings accounts because it looks higher than the base APR.
APR to APY Conversion Formula:
APY = (1 + (APR ÷ n))n − 1
Where n = number of compounding periods per year (12 for monthly, 365 for daily).

2. Real-World Comparison: How 24% APR Becomes 27.11% APY

Suppose a credit card charges a 24.00% APR compounded daily (n = 365). If you carry a balance for a full year without paying it off, you do not pay 24.00%—you pay 27.11% effective annual interest (APY) because interest is added to your balance every single day.

3. How to Use APR and APY to Your Advantage

  • When Borrowing (Mortgages, Auto & Personal Loans): Always compare loans by their effective APY and check whether origination fees are rolled into the disclosed APR.
  • When Saving or Investing: Look for accounts that compound daily or monthly and automatically reinvest dividends so your interest earns interest every month.
